Exercise 1

Welford’s Recurrence Formulas for $SS$ and Variance

Standard two-pass variance computation requires keeping all observations in memory, whereas the naive one-pass formula $\hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k x_i^2 - \bar{x}_k^2$ suffers from severe catastrophic cancellation in floating-point arithmetic whenever $\sigma \ll |\mu|$. Following the same recursive isolation technique used by D. E. Knuth for the sample mean, we derive B. P. Welford’s (1962) recurrence relations for the sum of squared deviations ($SS_k$) and the variance ($s_k^2$ and $\hat{\sigma}_k^2$).

1. Knuth’s Recurrence Approach for the Mean ($\bar{x}_k$)

Given $k$ observations $x_1, \dots, x_k$, isolate the newly arrived $x_k$ from the previous $k-1$ terms and substitute $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i = (k-1)\bar{x}_{k-1}$:

$$ \bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k x_i = \frac{1}{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i + x_k\right) = \frac{(k-1)\bar{x}_{k-1} + x_k}{k} = \frac{k\bar{x}_{k-1} - \bar{x}_{k-1} + x_k}{k} $$ $$ \implies \boxed{\bar{x}_k = \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}} $$

Let $\delta_k = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}$ denote the pre-update deviation and $\delta'_k = x_k - \bar{x}_k$ the post-update deviation. Using $\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} = \frac{\delta_k}{k}$, we immediately obtain the key identity linking the two deviations:

$$ x_k - \bar{x}_k = (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) - (\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) = \delta_k - \frac{\delta_k}{k} = \frac{k-1}{k}(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) = (k-1)(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) $$

2. Derivation of Welford’s Recurrence for the Sum of Squares ($SS_k$)

Define $SS_k = \sum_{i=1}^k (x_i - \bar{x}_k)^2$. Following Knuth’s approach, separate the $k$-th term and add/subtract the old mean $\bar{x}_{k-1}$ inside the summation over $i = 1, \dots, k-1$:

$$ SS_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_k)^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \Big[(x_i - \bar{x}_{k-1}) - (\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})\Big]^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)^2 $$

Expand the square across the first $k-1$ terms:

$$ SS_k = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2}_{=\,SS_{k-1}} -\, 2(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_{k-1})}_{=\,0} +\, (k-1)(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)^2 $$

Since $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(x_i - \bar{x}_{k-1}) = (k-1)\bar{x}_{k-1} - (k-1)\bar{x}_{k-1} = 0$, the cross-term vanishes identically. Now substitute the identity $(k-1)(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) = (x_k - \bar{x}_k)$ from Step 1 into $(k-1)(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2$:

$$ SS_k = SS_{k-1} + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)^2 $$

Factor out $(x_k - \bar{x}_k)$ from the last two terms and simplify $\big[(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)\big] = (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})$:

$$ \boxed{SS_k = SS_{k-1} + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) = SS_{k-1} + \frac{k-1}{k}(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2} $$

3. Derivation of Welford’s Recurrence for the Variance ($s_k^2$ and $\hat{\sigma}_k^2$)

We apply the same recursive substitution to express the variance at step $k$ as the variance at step $k-1$ plus a correction term:

(a) Unbiased Sample Variance $s_k^2 = \frac{SS_k}{k-1}$ (for $k \ge 2$, with $s_1^2 = 0$):

Substitute $SS_k = (k-1)s_k^2$ and $SS_{k-1} = (k-2)s_{k-1}^2$ into Welford’s $SS_k$ recurrence:

$$ (k-1)s_k^2 = (k-2)s_{k-1}^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) = (k-2)s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{k-1}{k}(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2 $$

Dividing both sides by $k-1$ and rewriting $\frac{k-2}{k-1}s_{k-1}^2 = s_{k-1}^2 - \frac{s_{k-1}^2}{k-1}$ yields the exact Knuth-style update:

$$ \boxed{s_k^2 = \frac{k-2}{k-1}s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2}{k} = s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) - s_{k-1}^2}{k-1}} $$

(b) Biased / Population Sample Variance $\hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \frac{SS_k}{k}$ (for $k \ge 1$):

Substitute $SS_k = k\hat{\sigma}_k^2$ and $SS_{k-1} = (k-1)\hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2$ and divide by $k$:

$$ \boxed{\hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \frac{k-1}{k}\hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k)}{k} = \hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) - \hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2}{k}} $$

Corresponding Code: 3 Lines of Core Logic

Whether maintaining $(\bar{x}_k, SS_k)$ or updating $(\bar{x}_k, s_k^2)$ directly inside the loop, the core recurrence requires only 3 lines of logic:

// Form A: Running Mean & Sum of Squares (SS_k)
for (const x of scores) {
    k += 1;
    const delta = x - mean;           // 1. x_k - mean_{k-1}
    mean += delta / k;                // 2. Knuth mean update
    ss   += delta * (x - mean);       // 3. Welford SS update
}
const s2 = ss / (k - 1); // Unbiased variance
const sigma2Hat = ss / k; // Biased variance
// Form B: Direct Running Mean & Variance Recurrence
for (const x of scores) {
    k += 1;
    const delta = x - mean;                        // 1. x_k - mean_{k-1}
    mean += delta / k;                             // 2. Knuth mean update
    s2 += k > 1 ? (delta*(x - mean) - s2)/(k-1) : 0; // 3. Variance update
}

4. Conclusions & Role in Inferential Statistics

1. Comparison of Empirical Averages with $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ (Unbiasedness)

Across the $m$ hackers, the average of the sampling means $\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m \bar{X}_j$ closely matches the theoretical mean $\mu$, and the average of the Bessel-corrected sampling variances $\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m S_j^2$ closely matches $\sigma^2$. This empirically confirms that $\bar{X}$ and $S^2 = \frac{SS_n}{n-1}$ are unbiased estimators: $$\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}] = \mu, \qquad \mathbb{E}[S^2] = \sigma^2.$$ Conversely, the unadjusted variance $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{SS_n}{n}$ has expectation $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 < \sigma^2$, systematically underestimating $\sigma^2$ (especially at small $n$) because estimating $\mu$ via $\bar{X}$ consumes $1$ degree of freedom.

2. Effect of Increasing $n$ on Estimator Variability (Consistency)

Increasing the number of systems $n$ attempted by each hacker causes the variability of both estimators across the $m$ hackers to shrink at rate $\mathcal{O}(1/n)$ for variances and $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})$ for standard errors: $$\text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0$$ $$\text{Var}(S^2) = \frac{1}{n}\left(\mu_4 - \frac{n-3}{n-1}\sigma^4\right) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0$$ Combined with unbiasedness, vanishing variance implies mean-squared error convergence ($\text{MSE} \to 0$) and convergence in probability ($\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$, $S_n^2 \xrightarrow{P} \sigma^2$): both estimators are consistent.

3. Why These Results Are Useful in Inferential Statistics

In real-world applications, we do not know the true parameters $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ of the data-generating distribution, nor can we observe $m$ parallel replications—we typically observe only one sample trajectory of size $n$. Unbiasedness and consistency guarantee that:

  • A single hacker’s $\bar{X}$ and $S^2$ provide reliable point estimates of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$.
  • We can quantify the uncertainty of $\bar{X}$ from a single sample by plugging $S$ into the Standard Error $\widehat{\text{SE}}(\bar{X}) = \frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}$.
  • By the Central Limit Theorem and Slutsky’s Theorem, the pivot $T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S / \sqrt{n}}$ converges to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ (or follows Student’s $t_{n-1}$ under normality), enabling confidence intervals $\bar{X} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}$ and hypothesis tests.
