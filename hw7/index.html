Part 1 • Probability Theory

1. Main Modes of Convergence & Their Roles in LLN and CLT

Unlike real sequences $\{a_n\}$, a sequence of random variables $\{X_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ consists of measurable functions $X_n : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, \mathbb{P})$. Consequently, there is no single notion of limit: different modes capture progressively stronger guarantees on sample paths, probabilities, moments, or cumulative distributions.

1. Almost Sure Convergence ($X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X$)

The strongest pointwise notion: the set of sample paths $\omega \in \Omega$ on which $X_n(\omega)$ converges to $X(\omega)$ has probability $1$:

$\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\{\omega \in \Omega : \lim_{n \to \infty} X_n(\omega) = X(\omega)\right\}\right) = 1$

Role in Statistics: Governs the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) ($\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$ when $\mathbb{E}[|X_1|] < \infty$) and strong consistency of estimators (e.g., Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem for empirical CDFs). Once $n$ is large enough, a single observed trajectory stays permanently inside any $\varepsilon$-tube around $\mu$.

2. Mean-Square / $L^2$ Convergence ($X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X$)

Convergence in the Hilbert space $L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, \mathbb{P})$: the expected squared error (Mean Squared Error) vanishes as $n \to \infty$:

$\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X_n - X)^2\right] = 0$

Role in Statistics: Directly links to the Bias–Variance Decomposition $\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \text{Bias}^2(\hat{\theta}_n) + \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) \to 0$. Used to prove the $L^2$ Law of Large Numbers (when $\sigma^2 < \infty$), minimum-variance linear estimation, and stochastic calculus (Itô integrals).

3. Convergence in Probability ($X_n \xrightarrow{P} X$)

For every tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, the probability of observing a deviation larger than $\varepsilon$ at step $n$ vanishes:

$\forall \varepsilon > 0, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}\!\left(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon\right) = 0$

Role in Statistics: Governs the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) ($\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$, proved via Chebyshev's inequality when $\sigma^2 < \infty$, or characteristic functions when $\mathbb{E}[|X_1|] < \infty$) and defines weak consistency of estimators ($\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{P} \theta$). Unlike a.s. convergence, rare excursions beyond $\varepsilon$ can still recur infinitely often on a given path.

4. Convergence in Distribution ($X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$)

The weakest mode: only the cumulative distribution functions $F_{X_n}(x)$ converge to $F_X(x)$ at every continuity point $x$ of $F_X$:

$\lim_{n \to \infty} F_{X_n}(x) = F_X(x) \quad \forall x \text{ where } F_X \text{ is continuous}$

Role in Statistics: Governs the Central Limit Theorem (CLT): $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Notice that $X_n$ and $X$ need not even live on the same probability space—only their laws converge. Together with Slutsky's Theorem and the Delta Method, it underpins asymptotic confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

Why Does $\sqrt{n}$ Appear Everywhere? Variance Additivity & Independence

Consider a sample $X_1, \dots, X_n$ with common mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$. For the sample sum $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, the exact variance identity (Biénaymé's formula) is:

$$ \text{Var}(S_n) = \text{Var}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) + 2 \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) $$

1. Variance Additivity — Pairwise Uncorrelatedness vs. Full Independence: When all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairwise covariances vanish ($\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0$), variance becomes strictly additive, growing linearly as $\mathcal{O}(n)$:

$$ \text{Var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2 \implies \text{SD}(S_n) = \sigma\sqrt{n} \implies \text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \quad \text{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} $$

Crucially, pairwise uncorrelatedness is sufficient for variance additivity and for the $L^2$ / Chebyshev Weak LLN, whereas the Central Limit Theorem requires full independence (or strict weak-dependence/mixing conditions): independence is what allows the characteristic function of the sum to factorize into a product, $\varphi_{S_n}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^n \varphi_{X_i}(t) = [\varphi_X(t)]^n$, so that the second-order Taylor term $e^{-t^2/2}$ emerges in the limit.

2. Why $\sqrt{n}$ Is the Unique Critical Scaling Factor: To obtain a non-degenerate limiting distribution for the centered error $(\bar{X}_n - \mu)$, we scale it by $n^\gamma$ so its variance stabilizes to a positive finite constant. Since $\text{Var}\!\left(n^\gamma (\bar{X}_n - \mu)\right) = n^{2\gamma - 1}\sigma^2$:

$$ n^{2\gamma - 1}\sigma^2 \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \gamma < \frac{1}{2} \quad (\text{collapses to } 0, \text{ e.g. LLN at } \gamma = 0) \\ \sigma^2 \in (0, \infty) & \text{if } \gamma = \frac{1}{2} \quad (\text{CLT critical window: } \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)) \\ +\infty & \text{if } \gamma > \frac{1}{2} \quad (\text{fluctuations explode to } \pm\infty) \end{cases} $$

3. Consequence in Statistical Inference & Breakdown Under Dependence: Under i.i.d. sampling, confidence interval half-widths shrink as $z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$—meaning halving estimation uncertainty requires $4\times$ more data (diminishing returns). However, if observations share a positive pairwise correlation $\text{Corr}(X_i, X_j) = \rho > 0$ (e.g., common-shock dependence), the $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ covariance terms dominate:

$$ \text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\rho\sigma^2 \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} \rho\sigma^2 > 0 $$

Here the $\sqrt{n}$ law breaks completely: $\bar{X}_n$ fails even to be consistent! In autocorrelated time-series or network telemetry, the effective sample size $n_{\text{eff}} < n$ replaces $n$ in the standard error.

3. Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem & Posterior Updating

Conditional probability formalizes how new information reshapes uncertainty. Its inversion via Bayes' Theorem forms the mathematical engine of Bayesian statistical inference, turning unknown parameters into probability distributions that evolve coherently as data accumulates.

Conditional Probability: Probability Theory vs. Statistics

In Probability Theory (Axiomatic): On $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, \mathbb{P})$, for any event $B$ with $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$, the conditional probability restricts the sample space to $B$ and renormalizes total probability to $1$:

$\mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}$

In Statistics (Inference): Conditional probability connects hidden model parameters $\theta$ to observable data $\mathcal{D}$. Frequentist statistics fixes $\theta$ and studies only the sampling distribution $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$; Bayesian statistics treats $\theta$ itself as a random variable and uses conditioning to invert the arrow from $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$ to $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$.

Derivation of Bayes' Theorem

By symmetry of the multiplication rule $\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)\mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A)$, dividing by $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$ and expanding $\mathbb{P}(B)$ via the Law of Total Probability over a partition $\{H_i\}$ yields:

$\mathbb{P}(H_k \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D} \mid H_k)\,\mathbb{P}(H_k)}{\sum_i \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D} \mid H_i)\,\mathbb{P}(H_i)}$

For a continuous parameter $\theta \in \Theta$ with density $p(\theta)$:

$p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{\int_{\Theta} p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta')\,p(\theta')\,d\theta'} \propto \mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{D}) \cdot p(\theta)$

1. Prior Probability $p(\theta)$

Encodes epistemic uncertainty, physical constraints, or historical domain knowledge about $\theta$ before observing the current dataset $\mathcal{D}$.

  • Non-informative / Weakly informative: e.g., Uniform $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ or Jeffreys $\text{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)$ when prior knowledge is minimal.
  • Informative: Concentrates probability mass where previous studies or physical laws expect $\theta$ to lie.

2. Likelihood Function $\mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{D}) = p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$

Evaluates how probable the fixed observed dataset $\mathcal{D}$ is as a function of the candidate parameter $\theta$.

  • Crucial distinction: Viewed as a function of $\theta$, $\mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{D})$ is not a probability density over $\theta$ (it does not integrate to $1$).
  • Carries all information extracted from the sample; its maximum gives the Frequentist MLE $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}$.

3. Posterior Probability $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$

The updated probability distribution over $\theta$ after reweighting the prior by the likelihood and dividing by the marginal evidence $p(\mathcal{D})$.

  • Sequential Coherence: Today's posterior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}_1)$ automatically becomes tomorrow's prior when new data $\mathcal{D}_2$ arrives: $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2) \propto p(\mathcal{D}_2 \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}_1)$.
  • Yields direct probability statements (95% Credible Intervals) rather than repeated-sampling Frequentist CIs.

Canonical Continuous Example: Normal–Normal Precision Updating (Gaussian Mean Estimation)

Beyond proportions (Beta–Binomial below), suppose we observe i.i.d. continuous measurements $X_1, \dots, X_n \mid \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ (known variance $\sigma^2$, e.g., network latency jitter) and place a Gaussian prior $\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$. Defining precision as inverse variance ($\lambda_0 = 1/\tau_0^2$, $\lambda_{\text{data}} = n/\sigma^2$), Bayes' theorem yields a Gaussian posterior $\mu \mid \mathcal{D} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)$ where precisions add and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average:

$$ \frac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mu_n = \frac{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}\,\mu_0 + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}\,\bar{x}}{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}} $$

Note how a tighter prior ($\tau_0^2 \to 0$) pulls $\mu_n$ toward $\mu_0$ (acting exactly like $L_2$ / Ridge shrinkage in Machine Learning), while larger sample size ($n \to \infty$) drives $\mu_n \to \bar{x}$ and $\tau_n^2 \to \sigma^2/n$.

Applications & Key Advantages

  • Real-World Applications: Intrusion detection & spam filtering (Naïve Bayes / alert triage), sequential A/B testing, clinical diagnostics, Kalman/particle filtering, and probabilistic ML regularization (Gaussian prior ↔ $L_2$ Ridge; Laplace prior ↔ $L_1$ Lasso).
  • Exact Finite-Sample Inference: Provides full parameter uncertainty $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$ even at very small $n$, without relying purely on CLT asymptotics.
  • Online Sequential Updating & Intuition: Seamlessly assimilates streaming data and yields direct epistemic probabilities (e.g., $\mathbb{P}(\theta \in [a,b] \mid \mathcal{D}) = 0.95$).

Limitations & Influence on Conclusions

  • Small-Sample Prior Sensitivity: When $n$ is small, an overly confident mis-specified prior can dominate the likelihood and bias conclusions; assigning $p(\theta) = 0$ (violating Cromwell's Rule) makes data unable to ever revise that belief.
  • Asymptotic Dominance (Bernstein–von Mises): As $n \to \infty$, the likelihood sharpens at rate $1/\sqrt{n}$ and overwhelms any smooth non-zero prior, causing distinct priors to converge to the same posterior.
  • Computational Intractability: Outside conjugate pairs, computing the marginal evidence $\int p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta$ requires numerical sampling (MCMC / HMC) or Variational Inference.

4. Interactive Beta–Binomial Updating & Prior Sensitivity

Consider estimating an unknown success probability $\theta \in [0, 1]$ (e.g., vulnerability exploit rate or click-through probability). Pairing a conjugate $\text{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$ Prior with a $\text{Binomial}(n, k)$ Likelihood yields an exact analytical $\text{Beta}(\alpha_0 + k, \beta_0 + n - k)$ Posterior:

$p(\theta \mid k, n) \propto \underbrace{\theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k}}_{\text{Likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{\theta^{\alpha_0 - 1} (1 - \theta)^{\beta_0 - 1}}_{\text{Prior}} = \theta^{(\alpha_0 + k) - 1}(1 - \theta)^{(\beta_0 + n - k) - 1} \implies \theta \mid \mathcal{D} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0 + k,\; \beta_0 + n - k)$

Its posterior mean decomposes algebraically into a convex combination of the prior mean and the Frequentist MLE $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = k/n$, showing directly how prior pseudo-counts $(\alpha_0 + \beta_0)$ compete with observed sample size $n$:

$$ \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid \mathcal{D}] = \frac{\alpha_0 + k}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n} = \underbrace{\frac{\alpha_0 + \beta_0}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n}}_{w_{\text{prior}}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\alpha_0}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0}}_{\text{Prior Mean}} + \underbrace{\frac{n}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n}}_{w_{\text{data}}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{k}{n}}_{\text{MLE}} $$
