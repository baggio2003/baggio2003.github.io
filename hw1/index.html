Homework 1: Private Key → Public Key (ECDLP)

Scalar multiplication over the toy elliptic curve $y^2 \equiv x^3 + 7 \pmod{17}$, discrete torus visualization, and statistical evaluation of one-way cryptographic properties.

1. Mathematical Model & Ethereum Curves

In Ethereum, public key derivation from a private key $k$ is governed by elliptic-curve scalar multiplication over the standardized curve secp256k1:

$$Q = k \cdot G = \underbrace{G + G + \dots + G}_{k \text{ times}}$$

While the forward computation $k \to Q$ is deterministic and efficient via the double-and-add algorithm, recovering $k$ given only $Q$ and $G$ requires solving the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP), which is computationally infeasible for large prime fields ($p \approx 2^{256}$).

For educational exploration, we define a toy curve sharing the exact Weierstrass coefficients of secp256k1 ($a = 0, b = 7$), projected over the small finite field $\mathbb{F}_{17}$:

$$E(\mathbb{F}_{17}): y^2 \equiv x^3 + 7 \pmod{17}, \quad G = (6, 6) \text{ or } G = (1, 5)$$

4. Statistical Exploration of the Multiples Subgroup

Empirical evaluation of scalar multiples $k \in \{1, \dots, 18\}$. We analyze the distribution of coordinates, bit parity, apparent uniformity, and the absence of linear correlations:

Statistical Findings & Properties

  • 1. Coordinate Frequencies & Quadratic Residues: The coordinates $x \in \mathbb{F}_{17}$ are not uniformly distributed over all field elements $\{0, \dots, 16\}$. For any given $x$, the value $z = x^3 + 7 \pmod{17}$ is either a quadratic residue (yielding two symmetric solutions $\pm y$), zero (one solution $y = 0$), or a non-residue (zero solutions). Hence, some coordinates occur twice while others never appear on the curve.
  • 2. Bit Distribution (LSB Parity): The least significant bit (even vs. odd parity) is evenly balanced across the generated coordinates, demonstrating empirical 50/50 entropy without systemic parity bias.
  • 3. Apparent Uniformity vs. Group Law: While points appear scattered unpredictably across the grid, their distribution is strictly constrained by the algebraic group law $E(\mathbb{F}_{17})$.
  • 4. Input-Output Correlation ($k \mapsto x(kG)$): The mapping shows a pseudo-random walk pattern. The linear correlation coefficient is approximately zero ($r \approx 0$), proving that knowing $k$ offers no continuous or monotonic hint regarding the magnitude of $x_Q$.
  • 5. Avalanche-like Hopping: Incrementing $k$ by 1 ($k \to k + 1$) causes large, discontinuous jumps across the field. However, this is algebraic step-hopping governed by $Q_{k+1} = Q_k + G$, rather than an uncorrelated bit-avalanche.

5. Critical Discussion: One-Way Functions vs. Cryptographic Hash Functions

While both elliptic-curve scalar multiplication and cryptographic hash functions (such as SHA-256 or Keccak-256) are formally classified as One-Way Functions (OWFs), their mathematical design fulfills fundamentally distinct roles:

Property Elliptic-Curve Scalar Multiplication ($k \mapsto kG$) Cryptographic Hash Function ($\text{Hash}(m)$)
Algebraic Homomorphism Preserved: $(k_1 + k_2)G = k_1 G + k_2 G$. Fundamental for public-key cryptography and ECDSA signatures. Destroyed: $\text{Hash}(m_1 + m_2) \neq \text{Hash}(m_1) + \text{Hash}(m_2)$. Any linearity is a fatal security flaw.
Avalanche Effect (SAC) Partial / Algebraic: Coordinates jump non-linearly, but outputs are deterministically bound by $Q_{k+1} = Q_k + G$. Strict: Inverting a single input bit flips approximately 50% of all output bits with zero residual correlation.
Domain & Compression Bijective / Subgroup Mapping: Maps a 256-bit scalar into a 256-bit point. No entropy loss or arbitrary compression. Lossy Compression: Maps arbitrary-length inputs into a fixed 256-bit digest, inducing theoretical collisions.
Core Purpose Asymmetric key pairs, Diffie-Hellman key exchange, digital signatures. Data integrity, commitments, Proof-of-Work, content addressing.
