Correlation, Linear Regression & Independence

Pearson's $r$, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Derivation for $a$ and $b$, Dual Regression Lines ($Y|X$ & $X|Y$), Geometric Mean Verification, and Proof that Independence Implies Uncorrelation

Part 1 • Linear Association

1. Covariance & Pearson's Linear Correlation Coefficient $r$

Given a bivariate dataset $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ (or random variables $X, Y$ with finite, non-zero variances $\sigma_X^2, \sigma_Y^2 \in (0, \infty)$), covariance measures the joint linear co-movement of deviations from their respective means, while Pearson's correlation coefficient standardizes covariance into a dimensionless index on $[-1, 1]$.

Covariance (Population & Sample)

$\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\right] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$
$s_{XY} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$

Positive $s_{XY}$ indicates that above-average $X$ values tend to pair with above-average $Y$ values; negative $s_{XY}$ indicates inverse co-movement. Because $s_{XY}$ inherits the product of units of $X$ and $Y$, its raw magnitude cannot be compared across scales.

Pearson's $r$ & Geometric Meaning ($\cos\theta$)

$r = \frac{s_{XY}}{s_X s_Y} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$

Notice that dividing by $n$ or $n - 1$ cancels identically between numerator and denominator. Letting $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (x_1 - \bar{x}, \dots, x_n - \bar{x})^\top$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = (y_1 - \bar{y}, \dots, y_n - \bar{y})^\top$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, Pearson's $r$ is literally the cosine of the angle between the centered data vectors:

$r = \frac{\langle \tilde{\mathbf{x}}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}} \rangle}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2 \, \|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2} = \cos\theta \in [-1, 1]$

Fundamental Algebraic Properties of Pearson's $r$:

  • Cauchy–Schwarz Bound ($|r| \le 1$): $|\langle \tilde{\mathbf{x}}, \tilde{\mathbf{y}} \rangle| \le \|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2 \|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2$, with equality $|r| = 1$ if and only if $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = b\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ for some $b \neq 0$ (all points lie strictly on a non-horizontal, non-vertical line $y = a + bx$).
  • Affine Invariance: For any $c_1, c_2 \neq 0$ and shifts $d_1, d_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, $r(c_1 X + d_1,\; c_2 Y + d_2) = \operatorname{sgn}(c_1 c_2)\,r(X, Y)$.
  • Symmetry & Linearity Limitation: $r(X, Y) = r(Y, X)$, and $r$ detects linear association only—strong nonlinear relationships (e.g., $Y = X^2$) can yield $r = 0$.
Part 2 • Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)

2. Solving the Linear Regression Problem for $a$ and $b$

To predict $Y$ as a linear function $\hat{y}_i = a + b x_i$, Ordinary Least Squares chooses the intercept $a$ and slope $b$ that minimize the Sum of Squared vertical Residuals (RSS):

Step 1 — Objective Function & Normal Equations:

$$S(a, b) = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(y_i - a - b x_i\bigr)^2.$$

Setting both first partial derivatives to zero yields the Normal Equations:

$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial a} = -2\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - a - b x_i) = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad n a + b\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \boxed{\hat{a} = \bar{y} - \hat{b}\bar{x}}$$

Immediately, $\hat{a} + \hat{b}\bar{x} = \bar{y}$: the OLS regression line always passes through the sample centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, and the residuals sum identically to zero ($\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0$).

Step 2 — Closed-Form Solution for the Slope $\hat{b}$:

Substituting $\hat{a} = \bar{y} - \hat{b}\bar{x}$ into the second normal equation $\frac{\partial S}{\partial b} = -2\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \hat{a} - \hat{b}x_i) = 0$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\bigl((y_i - \bar{y}) - \hat{b}(x_i - \bar{x})\bigr) = 0.$$

Since $\bar{x}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y}) = 0$ and $\bar{x}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$, we may replace the leading $x_i$ with $(x_i - \bar{x})$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) - \hat{b}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \boxed{\hat{b}_{Y|X} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2} = r\,\frac{s_Y}{s_X}}$$

Step 3 — Second-Order Global Optimality (Hessian Check):

$$H(a, b) = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 S}{\partial a^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2 S}{\partial a\,\partial b} \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial^2 S}{\partial b\,\partial a} & \dfrac{\partial^2 S}{\partial b^2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2n & 2\sum x_i \\ 2\sum x_i & 2\sum x_i^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(H) = 4n\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 4n^2 s_X^2 > 0.$$

Because $\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial a^2} = 2n > 0$ and $\det(H) > 0$ whenever $s_X^2 > 0$, $S(a, b)$ is strictly convex and $(\hat{a}, \hat{b})$ is its unique global minimum.

Relationship Between $r$ and Regression ($R^2 = r^2$)

Rewriting $\hat{y} = \bar{y} + r\frac{s_Y}{s_X}(x - \bar{x})$ in standardized $z$-scores reveals Galton's regression toward the mean:

$\dfrac{\hat{y} - \bar{y}}{s_Y} = r \cdot \dfrac{x - \bar{x}}{s_X}$

Proof of $\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}$ and $R^2 = r^2$: Writing $y_i - \bar{y} = (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) + e_i$, squaring, and summing gives a cross-term $2\sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})e_i = 2\hat{b}_{Y|X}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})e_i = 0$ by the second Normal Equation, hence $\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}$. Since $\hat{y}_i - \bar{y} = \hat{b}_{Y|X}(x_i - \bar{x})$:

$\mathrm{ESS} = \hat{b}_{Y|X}^2 \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \left(\dfrac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2}\right)^2 n s_X^2 = r^2 (n s_Y^2) = r^2\,\mathrm{TSS} \;\Longrightarrow\; R^2 = \dfrac{\mathrm{ESS}}{\mathrm{TSS}} = r^2$

Thus $r^2$ is the exact fraction of the variance of $Y$ explained linearly by $X$.

Both Regression Lines & Geometric Mean Identity

Swapping the roles of response and predictor minimizes horizontal squared errors $\sum (x_i - a_{X|Y} - b_{X|Y} y_i)^2$, yielding the second regression line $L_{X|Y}$:

$L_{Y|X}:\; \hat{y} = a_{Y|X} + b_{Y|X} x, \quad b_{Y|X} = \dfrac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2} = r\dfrac{s_Y}{s_X}$
$L_{X|Y}:\; \hat{x} = a_{X|Y} + b_{X|Y} y, \quad b_{X|Y} = \dfrac{s_{XY}}{s_Y^2} = r\dfrac{s_X}{s_Y}$

Because $s_X^2, s_Y^2 > 0$, both slopes always share the exact sign of $s_{XY}$, so $b_{Y|X}\cdot b_{X|Y} \ge 0$ always. Taking the signed square root preserves negative correlations ($r < 0$):

$b_{Y|X} \cdot b_{X|Y} = \left(\dfrac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2}\right)\!\left(\dfrac{s_{XY}}{s_Y^2}\right) = \dfrac{s_{XY}^2}{s_X^2 s_Y^2} = r^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{r = \operatorname{sgn}(s_{XY})\sqrt{b_{Y|X} \cdot b_{X|Y}}}$

Both lines intersect at $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$. They coincide iff $|r| = 1$ ($\phi_{\mathrm{std}} = 0^\circ$) and are perpendicular ($y = \bar{y}$ vs.\ $x = \bar{x}$) iff $r = 0$ ($\phi_{\mathrm{std}} = 90^\circ$); in standardized coordinates, $\tan\phi_{\mathrm{std}} = \frac{1 - r^2}{2|r|}$.

Streaming $\mathcal{O}(1)$ Connection (Welford / Pébay Online Co-Moment, cf. Homework 4): Rather than storing all $n$ pairs or risking catastrophic cancellation in $\sum x_i y_i - n\bar{x}\bar{y}$, the centered co-moment $C_n = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}_n)(y_i - \bar{y}_n)$ updates in a single pass alongside Welford's $M_{2,X}$ and $M_{2,Y}$ via $C_n = C_{n-1} + (x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1})(y_n - \bar{y}_n)$, yielding $r = C_n / \sqrt{M_{2,X} M_{2,Y}}$, $\hat{b}_{Y|X} = C_n / M_{2,X}$, and $\hat{b}_{X|Y} = C_n / M_{2,Y}$ in strictly $\mathcal{O}(1)$ memory.

Part 4 • Mathematical Proof & Counterexamples

4. Proof That Independence Implies Uncorrelation (and Why the Converse Fails)

Theorem (Independence $\implies$ Uncorrelation): Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent random variables with finite, strictly positive variances $0 < \mathrm{Var}(X), \mathrm{Var}(Y) < \infty$. Then $\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ and consequently $\rho(X, Y) = 0$.

Proof (Discrete Case): Independence means that the joint probability mass function factorizes for every $(x, y)$ as $p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)$. Computing $\mathbb{E}[XY]$ (absolutely convergent since $\mathbb{E}[|XY|] \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Y^2]} < \infty$ by Cauchy–Schwarz):

$$\mathbb{E}[XY] = \sum_{x} \sum_{y} x y \, p_{X,Y}(x, y) = \sum_{x} \sum_{y} x y \, p_X(x) p_Y(y) = \left(\sum_{x} x\,p_X(x)\right)\!\left(\sum_{y} y\,p_Y(y)\right) = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y].$$

Proof (Continuous Case): Independence means that the joint density factorizes almost everywhere as $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)$. By Fubini–Tonelli's Theorem (since $\mathbb{E}[|X|] < \infty$ and $\mathbb{E}[|Y|] < \infty$):

$$\mathbb{E}[XY] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x y \, f_X(x) f_Y(y)\,dx\,dy = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f_X(x)\,dx\right)\!\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y\,f_Y(y)\,dy\right) = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y].$$

Conclusion (General Measure-Theoretic Form): In general, independence implies $\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(Y)] = \mathbb{E}[g(X)]\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]$ for all integrable Borel functions $g, h$. Setting $g(x) = x - \mu_X$ and $h(y) = y - \mu_Y$ yields:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \rho(X, Y) = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} = 0. \qquad \square$$

Why the Converse Fails: $\rho = 0 \;\not\!\!\implies$ Independence

Counterexample (Symmetric Parabola): Let $X$ be symmetrically distributed around $0$ with $\mathbb{E}[X^4] < \infty$ (e.g., $X \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(-1, 1)$ or $X \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ with equal probability $1/3$), and set $Y = X^2$.

$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \rho(X, Y) = 0$

Yet $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$: knowing $X = 0$ forces $Y = 0$ with probability $1$, whereas unconditionally $\mathbb{P}(Y = 0) < 1$. Uncorrelation only rules out a linear trend—never nonlinear dependence!

The Notable Exception: Jointly Gaussian $(X, Y)$

When $(X, Y)$ follows a Bivariate Normal distribution $\mathcal{N}_2(\mu_X, \mu_Y, \sigma_X^2, \sigma_Y^2, \rho)$, the joint density is determined completely by means, variances, and $\rho$:

$\rho = 0 \iff f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y) \iff X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$

Subtle Trap: Marginal normality alone is not sufficient! If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = S \cdot X$ where $S \in \{-1, +1\}$ is an independent Rademacher sign, then both $X$ and $Y$ are $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ with $\rho(X, Y) = 0$, yet $|Y| = |X|$ (strongly dependent, not jointly Gaussian!).
